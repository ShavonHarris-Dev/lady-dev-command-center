# Where am I getting screwed?

That is the question. Not "summarize this contract." Not "is this standard." Contracts are written by the side that drafted them, for the side that drafted them, and the interesting parts are the ones designed not to be noticed.

## Before anything else

**This is not legal advice, and this pass does not replace a lawyer.** What it does is make the lawyer cheaper and faster: they get a document with the concerning clauses already flagged, instead of billing hours to find them. Say this plainly in every output — not as a disclaimer at the bottom, but as the frame.

For anything material — a real client, a real brand deal, real money — the flow is: **agent pass → human read → lawyer.** Never agent → signature.

## Read for these first

Work through the document specifically hunting for:

**Money**
- When do they actually pay? Net 30, net 60, net 90, or "upon acceptance" — which can mean never
- What triggers payment, and who decides the trigger has happened?
- Late fees, or their absence
- Who eats expenses, taxes, transaction fees, currency conversion
- Is anything contingent on something outside your control?

**Exit**
- Can they terminate for convenience? Can you? Are those symmetrical? (They usually are not.)
- What happens to money already earned but unpaid at termination
- Notice periods — and whether they run against you differently than against them
- Auto-renewal, and the window to escape it

**Ownership and rights**
- Who owns what you make? For how long? In what media?
- Perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, sublicensable — every one of those words costs you something
- Can they reuse your content or likeness after the deal ends?
- Are you signing away anything about work you made *before* this deal?

**Restrictions**
- Exclusivity — how broad, how long, and does it block work you'd actually want?
- Non-compete, non-solicit — scope and duration
- Approval rights over what you say publicly
- Anything limiting other brand deals in the category

**Risk**
- Indemnification — who covers whom, and is it one-directional?
- Liability caps — is yours capped? Is theirs?
- Warranties you're making that you can't actually guarantee
- Dispute resolution: arbitration, jurisdiction, who pays legal fees

**The quiet ones**
- Anything defined vaguely so they interpret it later ("satisfactory," "commercially reasonable," "as needed")
- Obligations with no deadline attached
- Anything that survives termination
- Rights they can assign to a third party without asking you

## How to report

Ranked by what it could cost, worst first. For each:

- **The clause** — quoted, with its location
- **What it actually means** in plain language
- **What it could cost** — the realistic bad scenario, not the theoretical one
- **What to ask for instead** — specific replacement language where you can

Then three summary sections:

1. **Walk-away items** — things that should be fixed or this isn't worth signing
2. **Worth negotiating** — real value, but not deal-breakers
3. **Standard and fine** — so they know what you checked and cleared, not just what alarmed you

## Rules

**Do not pad the list.** Flagging fifteen things when three matter buries the three. If the contract is genuinely fair, say it's genuinely fair — an agent that finds problems in everything gets ignored exactly when it finds a real one.

**Asymmetry is the tell.** Most unfair terms aren't obviously outrageous; they're a right one party has and the other doesn't. Read every clause twice — once as written, once with the parties swapped. If it reads absurd reversed, that's your finding.

**Silence counts.** What a contract *doesn't* say is often the problem. No payment deadline, no termination rights for you, no cap on your liability.

**Say what you're unsure about.** Confident wrong readings of a contract are genuinely dangerous. Flag anything a lawyer needs to look at specifically.

## Also run it the other way

On documents *you* are sending — proposals, your own terms, client agreements — flip the question: **"where is the other side getting screwed?"**

Not out of charity. A contract that's obviously one-sided gets negotiated, delayed, or resented, and it costs more time than it saves.

## Log it

File the review in the Notion Research Log with the counterparty, the date, and what you flagged. Patterns matter — the same agency using the same aggressive clause across three deals is a fact worth having before the fourth conversation.
